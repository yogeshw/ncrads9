Coordinates and WCS

Every pixel has several addresses at once, and NCRADS9 shows them together in the information panel above the image.

The four coordinate systems

SystemWhat it counts
ImagePixels in the array as loaded, from 1 at the bottom left, as FITS counts them.
PhysicalPixels in the original detector frame, before any trimming or binning — what LTV/LTM in the header describe.
Amplifier and DetectorThe readout's own coordinates, for instruments whose headers carry them.
WCSPosition on the sky, from the header's world coordinate solution.

View chooses which of these the panel shows, and Multiple WCS shows a second solution where a header carries one.

Sky systems and formats

The WCS menu picks the system — FK5, FK4, ICRS, Galactic, Ecliptic — and the format, Sexagesimal or Degrees. The choice applies to the readout, to region files written in world coordinates, and to what XPA reports.

ICRS and FK5 differ by tens of milliarcseconds; FK4 is a different equinox and differs by arcminutes. If positions from NCRADS9 disagree with a catalogue at that level, check that both are in the same system before looking for a bug.

Which way is north

The panner draws a compass for north and east, taken from the WCS and rotated with the display, so it stays right when you rotate or flip the image. WCS → Show Direction Arrows additionally draws the compass over the image itself; DS9 puts it only in the panner, and this toggle defaults off.

The coordinate grid

Analysis → Coordinate Grid draws a graticule over the image, and Coordinate Grid Parameters controls it in detail: which system, the spacing, whether the labels sit inside or outside the frame, the fonts, the colours, the title and the axis titles. The grid is drawn from the WCS, so it curves where the projection curves.

Rotate or flip the frame and the grid follows the sky, not the pixels: the lines turn with the picture, while the border, the numbers and the tick marks stay square to the window, so the numbers are always read along the edges of the display. A rotated image sits inside a larger upright box, and the grid covers the whole of it -- including the corners beyond the data -- which is why the spacing may widen when you turn a frame.

Pan, zoom and rotate

  • Zoom → Center Image re-centres; middle-click centres on a point.
  • Zoom In, Zoom Out, Zoom Fit (Ctrl+0) and the fixed steps from 1/32 to 32. An image loads fitted to the window and, while fitted, stays fitted: resizing the window rescales the image to fill it. Choosing a zoom of your own stops that, so the scale you set is the scale you keep until you fit again. When frames are tiled, the whole mosaic refits to the window.
  • Invert X, Invert Y, Invert XY flip the display. FITS counts rows from the bottom and screens from the top, so an image with no WCS can look flipped compared with another program's default.
  • 0, 90, 180, 270 Degrees rotate it.
  • Zoom → Align rotates the image so that north is up according to its WCS.
  • Pan Zoom Rotate Parameters sets all of them by typing numbers — including panning to a position in world coordinates, which is how you go to a named source.

The panner, the magnifier and any regions, contours or grid all follow the rotation, so what you measure is what you see.

Resolving a name

Analysis → Name Resolution looks a source name up with SIMBAD or NED and gives you its coordinates, which you can then pan to.
